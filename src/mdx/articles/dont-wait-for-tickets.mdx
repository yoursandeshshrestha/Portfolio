At early-stage startups, job titles often overlap. You might join as a frontend developer, but you could end up writing backend code, fixing tests or setting up a build script. That is normal. From what I have seen, what matters most is how you work, not your title. The developers who make a difference are the ones who do not wait to be told what to do. They look around, they pick something useful, and they get started.

Here are some things I have learned from doing that.

## 1. If You See Something Broken, Fix It

When I started, I used to ask, "What should I work on next?"

But most people were too busy to reply.

So I looked for a bug, fixed it, and pushed it.

No one asked me to, but they noticed. That is when I understood how important it is to take responsibility.

## 2. If Something Blocks You, Try to Unblock It

Once, I needed more data from an API.

The backend developer was busy, so I asked if I could update it myself. They said yes, so I did it.

This helped me move faster, and it saved their time too.

If you can fix it without making things worse, you should.

## 3. Focus on What Matters, Not Just What Is Assigned

Completing tasks is good, but that does not always mean you are adding value.

I try to ask, "Is this useful for the product or the team?"

If not, I move to something that is.

It is better to fix one real problem than to finish five small tasks that do not help much.

## 4. Take the Tasks No One Else Picks

Some bugs are hard to understand. Some parts of the codebase are messy.

But when I started taking those on, I learned fast. I understood how the system worked, and people started trusting me with more.

The hard tasks helped me grow.

## 5. If You Want Trust, Be Reliable

I like flexible hours, but I learned that comes after showing results.

If I ship clean code, give updates, and solve problems without asking for help every time, people trust me to work in my own way.

Freedom comes after trust, not before.

## 6. Your Role Is a Starting Point, Not a Limit

I was hired as an Intern. But I worked so hard to learn the system, and I was able to show results. That is how I was getting promoted so quickly, everyone was surprised.

## 7. You Do Not Need to Talk the Most

I am not the loudest in meetings. But I help people, I fix issues without being asked, and I write things down when I know it will help others later.

People remember who helps, even if they are quiet.

## 8. Ask Why Before You Build

In the beginning, I used to build things exactly as written.

Now, I ask "Why are we doing this?"

It helps catch bad ideas early. It helps improve the solution before time is wasted.

The best developers are not the ones who build fast. They are the ones who ask the right questions first.

---

Startups are busy, and plans change a lot.

That is why it is useful to take initiative. If something needs doing and you can do it, pick it up and get it done.

That is how I have learned the most, and how I have added the most value.
